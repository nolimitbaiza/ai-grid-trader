AI Grid Trader: teaching a battery when not to trade.

An AI agent that trades a 100 MWh grid battery on a full year of real wholesale electricity prices in California, Germany and South Australia. It only sees a forecast, it pays for its own battery wear, and it is scored against both a simple trading rule and the theoretical best.

01Problem

Cheap power, expensive cycles.

Batteries are what let a grid run on more solar and wind. They soak up power when it's plentiful and cheap, and give it back at the evening peak. The owner earns the price gap.

But every charge and discharge wears out the cells, and nobody knows tomorrow's prices for sure. The two obvious strategies both fail:

  • Trade every price wiggle, and the wear eats the profit and shortens the battery's life.
  • Follow a simple rule (buy below the recent average, sell above it), and you trade far too often on spreads that don't cover the wear.

So the question I set out to answer: on real prices, with only a forecast and a fair charge for wear, how much can a battery actually earn, and how close is that to the best possible?

02Approach

Plan like an operator, pay for wear.

A decision every 15 minutes

I framed dispatch as a Markov decision process. The state is (time, state of charge, current mode), the action is how much to charge, discharge or hold, and the reward is:

reward = arbitrage cash − battery wear − micro-cycling penalty

The decision table is solved exactly with dynamic programming (Bellman updates). That's the same answer a fully trained Q-learning agent converges to, but in milliseconds and identical on every run, which matters when you're showing it to someone.

Wear is a cost, not an afterthought

Every MWh moved is charged its share of a cycle's wear: about €400 per cycle in Germany ($500 in California, A$900 in South Australia), the price of new cells for the capacity a cycle uses up. A trade whose price gap doesn't beat that is simply skipped. A small penalty for starting a new charge or discharge run makes the agent commit to a few clean cycles instead of chasing noise.

No peeking at the future

  1. 1Get a fresh 24-hour price forecast
  2. 2Re-plan the whole day with dynamic programming
  3. 3Execute only the next hour
  4. 4Score it on the prices that actually happened

The simulated forecast behaves like a real one: it misses short spikes, flattens peaks and dips further ahead, and its errors are correlated. A slider in the demo sets how wrong it is.

All three strategies use the same accounting, on a full calendar year of real 2025 prices in 15-minute steps: CAISO SP15 (California), Germany-Luxembourg day-ahead via Energy-Charts, and AEMO SA1 (South Australia).

What I took away

  • A forecast is enough. Seeing only a forecast, the agent still captures 93–95% of what perfect hindsight could earn.
  • Knowing when not to trade is the edge. The rule-based bot loses money over a year in California and Germany, and over the project's life in all three markets, mostly by cycling on spreads that don't pay for the wear.
  • The market matters more than the algorithm. The same battery pays back in about 5 years in South Australia and doesn't cover its cost of capital in California at 2025 prices, even with the tax credit.
  • Wear pricing is visible. Drag the demo's wear cost to zero and the agent starts micro-cycling straight away.

8% discount rate, 2025 benchmark installed costs. Results are illustrative: forecast error is simulated, and past prices don't guarantee future revenue.

05What's next

What I'd build next.

  1. A real price forecaster. Forecast error is simulated today. Training a model on load, wind and solar forecasts would show how much of the remaining 5–7% gap to hindsight a better forecast closes.
  2. Physics-based degradation. Wear is a flat cost per cycle. Making it depend on depth of discharge, state of charge and temperature would change which cycles are worth taking.
  3. Co-optimising grid services. Power for frequency services is reserved up front. Deciding hour by hour whether a megawatt earns more in trading or in services should lift revenue.
  4. Financing and taxes. The lifetime model leaves out debt, taxes and residual value, which a real investment case needs.
